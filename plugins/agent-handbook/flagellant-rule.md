# The flagellant rule

In the sixteenth century, people walked from town to town whipping themselves in public. The
whipping was real. Nobody was lying about the pain, and nobody was pretending to be sorrier than
they were. It simply did not fix anything, and the crowd went home moved rather than informed.

That is the shape this rule is named after, and the reason the name is worth keeping: the failure
it describes is never dishonesty. It is a true thing done in place of a useful one.

## The half about writing

Self-blame is not honesty. It is an imitation of honesty, and it is expensive, because the apology
buries the fact underneath it. The reader finishes the paragraph knowing how sorry the writer is
and not knowing what broke.

An error is two sentences. One says what is wrong. One says what was done about it, or what will be
done and when. A third sentence about the person who made it is a performance, and the tell is that
it makes the reader feel something about the writer rather than know something about the work.

The uncomfortable part, and the reason this needs to be a standing rule rather than a good
intention: **the confession feels, from the inside, exactly like candour.** That is its mechanism,
not a side effect. Whoever is writing it experiences it as the most honest passage on the page,
which is precisely why "I would notice if I were doing this" is not a defence. The check has to be
external: after reading this, what can somebody verify? If the answer is *nothing, but they will
sense my sincerity*, it is a confession.

One exception worth stating, because the rule is otherwise easy to over-apply. A statement of fact
that happens to be unflattering is not an apology. *The check ran against the old version, so the
result does not apply* is a fact and it stays. Do not strip facts because they sound like
confession; strip the sentences that add nothing a reader can check.

## The half about instructions, which is the one that can actually be fixed

Someone who checks the same finished thing six times is not anxious. They are answering
instructions that pay for visible effort.

*Be thorough.* *Be careful.* *Double-check everything.* *Leave no stone unturned.* Each of these is
unfalsifiable, and that is the actual defect rather than the tone. Nobody can ever demonstrate
having complied with them, so the only safe reading is always *do more* — and none of the extra
work is correction, because re-reading a thing does not change it. Reward the appearance of
diligence and the appearance of diligence is exactly what arrives, on time, in quantity.

**The whip is in the instructions, not in the character.** This matters because characters cannot
be rewritten and instructions can. Asking a worker to relax is useless; telling them when they are
finished is not.

The replacement is a **stopping rule**, and it has three parts:

1. **Who owns the checking** — a person, a step, or something automatic. If nothing owns it, that is
   a finding. Say so rather than inventing an owner.
2. **How many passes** — a number.
3. **What ends a pass** — the observable condition, plus the instruction to stop and report when it
   holds.

The test for whether a line is a rule or a whip: given the text and the outcome, could a third party
say whether it was followed? A stopping rule survives that question. An exhortation never does.

The same shape appears at the other end, and the under-work reading is just as real as the
over-work one. A wrap-up summary is the place where "done" gets asserted without any accompanying
statement of what would still be outstanding if the writer were wrong — and it is unusual for the
list it names to be inaccurate. The list is accurate; it is simply not the same claim as *and there
is nothing else.* A true thing done in place of a useful one, arriving through the front door.

The mirror, then: a completion claim is a stopping condition exactly as *be thorough* is, and it
is unfalsifiable in exactly the same way. *Be thorough* runs long because nobody can show they have
done enough; *the work is finished* stops early because nobody can show there is anything left.
The cheap diagnostic is the same in both directions — before reporting completion, state what would
still be outstanding if you were wrong. If that sentence is easy to write, the report was not
honest; if it is hard, the report was probably not needed because the work was not done.

That is the missing half of the over-work rule, and it travels together for the same reason the
original did. The whip is in the instruction; the cure is in replacing the instruction, not in
asking the writer to be more careful.

## The sort that replaces every general instruction to be careful

Extra caution is a budget, not a virtue, and spreading it evenly is the same mistake as spending it
nowhere.

Sort the work by one line: **can a mistake be taken back?** Work where an error is recoverable gets
speed, because the cost of being wrong is one correction. Work where it cannot be undone keeps every
pass it had, and earns more.

Making that sort once, deliberately and in writing, replaces every vague instruction to be careful
scattered through a document. Where the sort is genuinely unclear, leave the caution in — unclear is
not the same as reversible.

This is the general form of a carve-out that every workshop discovers in its own vocabulary. The
vocabulary is local; the line is not.

## What this rule is not

It is **not permission to be sloppy.** Nothing here reduces the standard of the work; it removes
theatre from the reporting of the work and re-aims the caution at the places that need it.

It is **not about tone.** It catches one specific substitution — a true statement standing in for a
useful one — and has nothing to say about warmth, brusqueness or humour.

It **cannot tell you the right number of passes.** It can only ensure a number exists where an
exhortation used to. Whether one pass is enough is a judgement about the work, and it belongs to
whoever owns the work.

## Why it travels beside the walk

The other method in this handbook exists to find what is **missing**. The two are not filed together
for tidiness.

If finding something missing means blame, nobody walks honestly. They bring a tidier plan instead —
which is exactly the failure the walk was built to catch, arriving through the front door. And in
the other direction, without a method that produces a real standard, *stop apologising* is only
permission to do less.

One supplies the standard. The other makes it safe to meet it.
